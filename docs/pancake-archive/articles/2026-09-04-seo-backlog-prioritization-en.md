## Which backlog item should be prioritized first?

The item that combines the highest value with the lowest cost to implement — not the one at the top of the audit PDF.

Most SEO audits are ordered by severity or by the tool's own scoring system, not by what will actually move your traffic or your budget. A "critical" flag on a duplicate meta description is not more urgent than a missing H1 on your top landing page, even if the report lists it first. Before you touch a single line, re-sort the list yourself using two questions: what does this fix affect, and what does it cost to make.

This is exactly how software teams manage a [product backlog](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/5-key-factors-for-effective-product-backlog-prioritization) — value against cost, not order of discovery. SEO recommendations deserve the same discipline. An audit is a list of hypotheses. A backlog is a list of decisions.

## What is the 80/20 rule in SEO?

In practice, it means a small share of your recommendations — usually the technical fixes affecting your highest-traffic or highest-intent pages — will produce most of the visible movement, while the rest are cleanup.

Applied to a typical 40-item audit, this usually looks like:

- Indexation and crawlability issues on pages that already get traffic (broken canonicals, noindex tags left on by accident, redirect chains)
- Title and meta issues on pages ranking positions 4–15, where a rewrite can realistically move the needle
- Core Web Vitals or page-speed problems on templates used site-wide, not one-off pages
- Internal linking gaps to orphaned but relevant pages

Everything else — alt text on low-traffic images, minor schema tweaks, stylistic heading fixes — is real but not urgent. It belongs in the backlog, not in month one.

## How do you prioritize a backlog when you have no developer?

You separate the list by who can execute it, not just by impact — because a perfectly prioritized item you cannot implement is worth nothing.

This is the part most prioritization frameworks skip, and it's the part that actually matters if you're a solo marketing manager. Split your 40 items into three buckets:

1. **Content and copy** — title tags, meta descriptions, header structure, internal links. You can usually do this yourself in your CMS.
2. **Codebase and template changes** — canonical tags, structured data, redirect rules, Core Web Vitals fixes, XML sitemap logic. This requires someone who can open the theme files or the server config, not the WordPress editor.
3. **Decisions that need sign-off** — URL restructuring, migrating a subdomain, merging pages. These need a stakeholder conversation before anyone touches code.

Bucket 2 is where most audits die. It's also usually the bucket with the highest-value, highest-cost items — the ones your 80/20 analysis just told you to do first. If you have read this far looking for a prioritization method and you still don't have anyone to implement bucket 2, the method was never your bottleneck. ## Who prioritizes the product backlog — and who prioritizes yours?

In agile teams, that's the product owner's job. In an SME's SEO backlog, it's usually whoever is left holding the audit after the agency that produced it moves on — which, for most of our clients, is a solo marketing manager with no mandate over developer time.

That's a structural problem, not a skills problem. You can be excellent at prioritization — sorting by value, by cost, by dependency — and still watch the backlog sit untouched for two quarters, because prioritizing an item and implementing it are two different jobs, and only one of them requires code access.

If your organic traffic has been flat while your backlog grows, the prioritization framework isn't what's missing. What's missing is someone who will open the codebase this month, not someone who will re-rank the spreadsheet again.

## What to do once the backlog is sorted

Sorted is not done. Once you have your value/cost list and your who-can-do-it split, you have three real options, in order of how much of your own time they cost:

- **Do the content-bucket items yourself.** Titles, metas, internal links — this is legitimate, valuable work you can complete without a developer.
- **Hire a freelance developer per item.** Works for a one-off fix, but a 40-item backlog turns into 15 separate freelance engagements, invoices, and re-briefings — expensive in your time even when it's cheap in euros.
- **Put the technical bucket on a retainer that includes implementation**, not just advice. This is the option that actually clears bucket 2 every month instead of once.
